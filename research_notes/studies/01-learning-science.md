# Learning science of note-taking → features

Scope: what the evidence actually supports, and what NOTEAPP should build. Codebase state checked 2026-11-13 (`Packages/StudyKit`, `NoteApp/Study`, `NoteApp/AI/NoteAIPanel.swift`, `InkKit`, `NoteCore`).

Headline: the medium debate is weak; the **review loop** is strong. NOTEAPP already owns the medium (ink) and has a text-only SM-2 study module bolted on beside it. The gap worth closing is not "more handwriting" — it is getting **ink into retrieval practice and spaced review**.

## Key findings (each: finding — evidence strength — source ref)

- **The "pen is mightier than the keyboard" advantage is small, and its proposed mechanism did not survive replication.** Mueller & Oppenheimer (2014) reported a longhand advantage on conceptual questions in 3 studies; Morehead, Dunlosky & Rawson (2019) failed to reproduce it in two experiments (n=193, n=222), including against a no-notes group; Urry et al. (2021) direct replication (n=142) found no quiz difference and a mini meta-analysis of 8 similar studies was null. — *moderate (contested original; two failed replications)* — [1][2][3]
- **What does replicate: typing yields more words and more verbatim overlap.** Urry et al. reproduced the note-volume and verbatim-overlap differences decisively while the achievement difference vanished; the "verbatim overlap causes worse learning" link was not robust in their data. — *moderate* — [3][4]
- **Pooled across 24 studies (N≈3,005), handwritten notes still come out slightly ahead.** Flanigan, Wheeler, Colliot, Lu & Kiewra (2024) report Hedges' g = 0.248 for achievement and g = 0.919 for typed note volume. Treat 0.25 as "small, real-ish, not a design mandate". — *moderate (meta-analysis)* — [5]
- **The handwriting advantage is mostly a review advantage.** In the same meta-analysis the review subgroup was g = 0.421 [0.287, 0.554] versus g = 0.208 [0.124, 0.292] with no review; the review moderator was significant (Q(2)=7.94, p=.019). — *moderate (subgroup)* — [5]
- **Notes have two functions: encoding (the act) and external storage (the review).** Kiewra's framing, still the cleanest: taking notes helps independent of review; reviewing notes adds more. — *high (review)* — [6]
- **Notes-with-review beat no notes by a wide margin; structured/provided frameworks are the strongest intervention.** Kobayashi (2006), 33 studies: note-taking+review vs control d=.75, vs mental review d=.77; intervention vs no intervention d=.36, with "provided notes/framework" the biggest moderator (b=.82) and larger gains for lower-attaining students. — *moderate–high (meta-analysis)* — [7]
- **Testing beats restudying, and the gap grows with delay.** Roediger & Karpicke (2006): at 5 min restudy won (81% vs 75%), at 1 week testing won (56% vs 42%; Exp. 2 61% vs 40%). Rowland (2014) meta: g=0.50 over 159 effect sizes from 61 studies, g=0.69 when the retention interval was ≥1 day. — *high* — [8][9]
- **Recall formats beat recognition formats.** Rowland: cued recall g=0.61, free recall g=0.29, recognition g=0.29 in the full set; in the high-exposure subset free recall g=0.81 vs recognition g=0.36. Translation: don't build multiple-choice as the default review mode. — *high* — [9]
- **Spacing is the other high-utility technique, and the optimal gap scales with how long you need to remember.** Cepeda et al. (2006): 839 assessments, 317 experiments, 184 articles; optimal inter-study interval increases with retention interval; long-interval studies averaged ~15% benefit over massed. — *high* — [10]
- **Personalised scheduling beats one-size-fits-all spacing in a real course.** Lindsey, Shroyer, Pashler & Mozer (2014), semester-long middle-school Spanish: +16.5% retention over massed practice and +10.0% over generic spaced practice on a delayed cumulative exam. — *high (field RCT)* — [11]
- **SM-2 is a 1994 heuristic; FSRS-style schedulers are fitted to real review logs.** Woźniak & Gorzelańczyk tuned SM-2's expanding-interval formula on 7 volunteers and ~35,000 word pairs for a 95% retention target, with an ease factor and a 0–5 self-grade; Ye, Su & Cao trained a DSR-style model on 220M review logs and scheduled with a stochastic shortest path, reporting 12.6% over prior schedulers in their benchmark. Neither changes what a review is; they change when the card comes back. — *moderate* — [22][23]
- **Dunlosky et al.'s ratings are a useful product filter.** High utility: practice testing, distributed practice. Moderate: elaborative interrogation, self-explanation, interleaved practice. Low: summarization, highlighting, keyword mnemonic, imagery for text, rereading. — *moderate (narrative review with ratings)* — [12]
- **Interleaving is conditional, not a default.** Brunmair & Richter (2019), 59 studies / 238 effects: overall g=0.42, but g=0.34 for maths, g=0.67 for paintings/visual material, and g=−0.39 for word lists (blocking better). — *moderate (meta-analysis with strong moderators)* — [13]
- **Self-explanation prompts work, and "explain" / fill-in-the-blank prompts are the strong forms.** Bisra et al. (2018), 69 effects, N=5,917: overall g=0.55; vs no explanation g=0.67; vs being given an explanation g=0.35; "explain" prompts g=0.68, fill-in-the-blank g=0.90 (only 2 studies). — *moderate* — [14]
- **Concept mapping helps, but it is not a substitute for retrieval.** Nesbit & Adesope (2006), 67 effects / 55 studies / N=5,818: maps beat text, lectures and discussion, but were only slightly better than summaries/outlines. Karpicke & Blunt (2011) then showed retrieval practice beat elaborative concept mapping (M=.67 vs .45, d=1.50), even when the final test was drawing a map. — *moderate–high* — [15][16]
- **Drawing is a strong encoding boost — for simple items.** Wammes, Meade & Fernandes (2016), 7 experiments: drawn words recalled roughly twice as often as written ones, robust at 4 s per item, and not explained by elaboration, imagery or picture superiority. Caveat: single words, free recall. — *moderate* — [17]
- **Handwriting vs typewriting differs in brain connectivity, but that study measured no learning.** van der Weel & van der Meer (2024), 36 students, 256-channel EEG: handwriting produced widespread theta/alpha connectivity that typewriting did not. This is a neural correlate, not evidence of better retention; the authors' learning claim is inferential. — *low for a learning claim* — [18]
- **The "generation effect" underpins cloze and self-produced answers.** Bertsch, Pesta, Wiscott & McDaniel (2007), 445 effects / 86 studies / N=17,711: generating material beats reading it, g=.40 (cued recall .55). — *high* — [19]
- **Structured formats (Cornell, guided notes) are weakly evidenced, and the benefit lives in the prompts, not the lines.** Guided notes meta (Konrad, Joseph & Eveleigh 2009) covers only 8 studies / 125 students, mostly school-age and special education, and reports better note accuracy and quiz performance. A 2026 RCT (n=134, 5 weeks) found Cornell beat only the sentence-method control at 4-week retention, with no post-test differences. — *low–moderate* — [20][21]
- **Learners misjudge what works, so the app should schedule rather than ask.** In Karpicke & Blunt, 75% of participants believed concept mapping was as good or better than retrieval practice; 84% actually did better after retrieval. Repeated study also inflated confidence while producing worse retention (Roediger & Karpicke). — *moderate* — [8][16]

## What this means for NOTEAPP

1. **Do not build a "handwriting makes you learn" story.** The defensible claim is narrower: ink notes are as good or slightly better, they carry diagrams, and they are the medium the user already writes in. The product win is not the pen; it is that the pen's output never gets reviewed.
2. **The current architecture is the problem.** `StudyKit` is text-only (`StudyCard.question/answer: String`, one optional `imageAssetName`), lives in `NoteApp/Study/*` behind a Library sheet (`LibraryView.swift:467`), and is disconnected from the notebook. `sourceNotebookID`/`sourcePageID` exist on `StudyCard` but are never populated (`StudySetEditorView` writes cards with no source). Nothing in the notebook can create or open a card.
3. **Review, not capture, is where the effect size is.** Kobayashi's d≈.75 and Flanigan's review moderator both say the payoff arrives when notes are revisited. [5][7] Yet the app's only revisit surfaces are search, bookmarks (`PageBookmarks.swift`) and manual browsing.
4. **Retrieval must be effortful and free-recall-ish.** Rowland's recall>recognition split rules out MCQ-first design. [9] The existing four grade buttons map cleanly onto FSRS ratings, which is fortunate.
5. **SM-2 is legacy.** `SpacedRepetitionScheduler` implements 1994 SM-2 (7 volunteers, 35k items, fixed 95% retention target). FSRS-style DSR models are trained on 220M review logs and report ~12.6% over prior schedulers in their own benchmark, and Lindsey's personalised model beat generic spacing by 10 points in a classroom. [11][22][23] The UI (Again/Hard/Good/Easy) already fits FSRS's four ratings.
6. **Structure only helps if the structure is used.** The Cornell template (`PageTemplates.swift:44`, `TemplateRenderer.swift:157`) draws the lines but nothing consumes the cue column. Evidence for Cornell-as-lines is weak; evidence for generated cues + self-testing is good. [7][20][21] Make the cue column functional or it is decoration.
7. **Concealment is already a primitive.** `ElementContent.tape` is documented as "a band that hides what is under it until it is revealed", and Notability's own "active recall studying" flow is literally tape-over-terms. NOTEAPP has the object but not the study loop around it.
8. **Ink is addressable.** `StrokeRecord` has stable `id`, `mask`, `points` and per-stroke transform, and strokes are stored per page (`Page.strokes`). That makes "hide these strokes" and "crop these strokes into a card" tractable without a new document format. The blocker is that the lasso is PencilKit's built-in (`PencilCanvasView.swift:151` returns `PKLassoTool()`), so no selection set reaches app code.
9. **Don't reward rereading/highlighting.** Dunlosky rates both low. A "Review" button that opens the page for scrolling is the thing to avoid; every review entry point should ask a question first.
10. **Interleaving should be opt-in per set type.** Word lists got *worse* under interleaving; maths and visual categories got better. Default off, offer "Mix" for sets tagged as categories/maths.

## Top 3 to build first

**1. Lasso → handwritten card (#1).** This is the only feature here that no competitor can copy cheaply and that the evidence actually rewards: it converts the user's own ink into retrieval material, at the moment of encoding, which is where the drawing and generation effects live. It also fixes the structural hole — `StudyCard` already has `imageAssetName` and unused `sourceNotebookID`/`sourcePageID`, so the data model is half-built; the work is a selection model in `InkKit` and a crop renderer.

**2. Cloze by hiding ink (#2).** Retrieval practice on real notes, without asking the user to author text. `StrokeRecord.id` plus the existing tape reveal pattern means this can be done in the document format that already exists, and it makes the "review your notes" claim true for the first time.

**3. FSRS queue with one cross-set due list (#3 + #5).** The scheduling evidence (Cepeda, Lindsey) is the strongest in this document, the grade UI already matches FSRS's four ratings, and the current SM-2 implementation is a 1994 heuristic fitted to seven people. Doing #3 and #5 together turns Study from a place you visit into a daily queue that reaches you from the library and the notebook.

Deliberately not first: Cornell quizzes (#6) and interleaving (#11). Cornell's evidence is weak and the work is large; interleaving is actively harmful for word lists, so it needs set-level judgement that only exists after sets have types. Both are good second-wave features once cards can be made from ink and scheduled properly.

## Unverified

- **Mueller & Oppenheimer (2018) corrigendum** (*Psychological Science, 29*(9), 1565–1568). Widely cited as correcting error degrees of freedom and effect-size reporting, with the authors estimating much smaller effect sizes. I read only a secondary summary, not the corrigendum itself, so no corrected numbers are used above.
- **Morehead et al. (2019) sample sizes** (n=193 and n=222) come from a secondary summary, not the paper's own results section; the direction (no replication, including a no-notes control in Experiment 2) is corroborated by two independent summaries.
- **Yıldırım (2026)** reports no a priori power analysis and a single retention test 4 weeks after the intervention; the Cornell result is one pairwise comparison, not a general Cornell advantage.
- **Adesope, Trevisan & Sundararajan (2017), "Rethinking the use of tests: A meta-analysis of practice testing", *Review of Educational Research, 87*(3), 659–701.** Referenced in the literature I read but not opened this session; not used as a claim.
- **Askvik, van der Weel & van der Meer (2020), high-density EEG in 12-year-olds**, cited inside [18] as the earlier study behind it. Not opened separately.
- **Mueller & Oppenheimer's Study 3 effect size (d≈0.56)** appears only in secondary coverage; I did not open the original results section. The direction (conceptual-question advantage) is from the abstract of [1].
- **Voyer et al. (2022)** meta-analysis (reported by [21] as finding little longhand/digital difference) was not opened; the direction is only used as context in [21].
- **FSRS benchmark numbers** (Anki review-log benchmarks, `open-spaced-repetition/srs-benchmark`) are community engineering results, not peer-reviewed; only the KDD paper [23] is cited as evidence above.
