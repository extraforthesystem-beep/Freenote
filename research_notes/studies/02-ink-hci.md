# Digital Ink & Pen-Interaction HCI

Scope: stylus latency perception, prediction, palm rejection and hand posture, smoothing/beautification, shape recognition, pen gestures and scratch-out erase, pressure/tilt, mode switching, lasso, writing at zoom, glass vs paper friction, ink rendering quality, handedness.

Evidence strength: **Strong** = several controlled studies / replicated; **Moderate** = one controlled study, adequate n; **Weak** = single small study or non-peer-reviewed; **Product** = shipped-product documentation, no measurement.

## Key findings

**Latency and prediction**

1. Latency hurts *inking* far less than it hurts dragging. While inking, users notice delay at about **50 ms**; with the hand and stylus hidden from view the threshold rose to **~97 ms**, so the judgement is visual, made from the relative motion of nib and ink — **Moderate** (two psychophysical studies) — [5].
2. Stylus just-noticeable differences: ~1 vs 2 ms while dragging, **~7 vs 40 ms while scribbling**; commercial end-to-end latency at the time was 65–120 ms — **Moderate** — [4].
3. There is no "fast enough" plateau for direct touch: pointing performance kept improving down to 1 ms, i.e. latency degrades performance continuously and users compensate by slowing down — **Moderate** (single lab; the same group's later work extends it, not an independent replication) — [2], [3]. Users also prefer lower latency, and the improvement stayed noticeable below 10 ms — [1].
4. The sanctioned way to hide remaining latency is predicted touches: the system extrapolates the next samples, the app appends them temporarily and discards them on the next real event — **Strong** (platform contract) — [20]. Apple's guidance for custom ink engines is Metal rendering + predicted touches, and *no* transparent or blurred layers over the drawing surface — [21].
5. Smoothing and prediction are one problem: Google's ink-stroke-modeler smooths digitizer and human noise while preserving inflection points, and predicts forward specifically to pay back the lag smoothing adds — **Weak–Moderate** (documented engineering, not a study) — [22].

**Palm rejection and hand posture**

6. Contact-size heuristics are not enough. A probabilistic classifier over short time windows of touch area, velocity and distance to other touches reached **0.016 accidental palm inputs per pen stroke** while passing **98% of stylus inputs**, independent of orientation and handedness; deciding at touch-down was already 98.4% accurate, and reclassifying at 50 ms cut errors by 44% — **Moderate–Strong** — [7].
7. Rejection *regions* relative to the stylus work, but need a buffer because real grips put the nib below or to the right of the palm ("hooked"/inverted), and every region must be **mirrored for left-handers** — **Moderate** — [6].
8. Poor palm rejection changes posture rather than provoking complaints: users hover, "crab" the fingers, or knuckle-grip. Left-handers were more likely to use the knuckle grip; handedness was *not* significant for measured accuracy — **Moderate** (n=30, 14 left-handed) — [8].
9. Hand posture had little effect on measured accuracy, but participants *felt* less accurate with the hand in the air, and effect sizes suggested posture matters more for inking *speed* than visual feedback does — **Moderate** — [9]. One participant: "I never, ever want to buy a tablet that doesn't allow me to place my hand on the screen" — [9].

**Pen + touch and mode switching**

10. The division of labour that matches paper: **pen writes, touch manipulates, pen + touch yields new tools**. Touch on an object unifies selection with the pen's mode switch, and the muscular tension of holding is the "glue" that phrases a compound gesture — **Moderate** (observational design study) — [10].
11. Of five ink↔gesture switches, a **button pressed with the non-preferred hand was fastest**; **holding the pen still was significantly slower and more error-prone than every other technique**; the eraser end produced the fewest mode errors; pressure was promising but worse than the hand button — **Moderate** — [11].
12. Tilt/azimuth as mode switches beat timeout, barrel button and non-preferred-hand button in one study — **Weak** (single study, lower-tier venue) — [19].

**Shape recognition and beautification**

13. Template matching is cheap and accurate *for gestures*: the $1 recogniser reaches >97% with one template and 99% with three, and medium-speed gestures are recognised better than slow or fast ones — **Moderate** — [16]. $1 answers "which command was that", not "is this a square".
14. Geometric primitive recognition (PaleoSketch) classifies single strokes into eight primitives plus combinations, and merges multi-stroke primitives at a higher level — **Moderate** — [17]. That is the family `ShapeRecognizer` belongs to.
15. Beautification by "token means" — clustering the writer's own repeated letterforms and nudging each new token toward the cluster mean — preserves style and improves consistency, but **needs many samples of the same text** before it helps — **Moderate**, no independent replication — [15].

**Erase, lasso, selection**

16. Scratch-out (scribble over ink to delete it) is a long-standing pen convention: Windows Journal, OneNote, Microsoft Journal and GoodNotes all ship it, and all advise a *dense* scribble that covers the content — **Product** (no controlled evaluation found) — [23].
17. Lasso is the preferred multi-select for ink because tapping individual strokes is tedious; the cost is that selection and the following action need a delimiter, and pigtail/handle delimiters let a selection-action phrase be drawn without leaving ink mode — **Moderate** — [12].
18. Plain lasso is inefficient for large or clustered targets; inferring clusters during the gesture (ICE-Lasso) was significantly faster than lasso — **Moderate** — [13].

**Writing at zoom, surface, rendering**

19. A magnified writing region (focus + context) lets people write at a comfortable size and shrink the ink to page size; **2.0×** was found comfortable and still legible when shrunk — **Moderate** (design study, INTERACT) — [14].
20. Glass is measurably slipperier than paper: mean writing velocity **17.9 mm/s on paper vs 35.2 mm/s on a tablet**; children's handwriting quality was worse on the tablet, and adults adapted by changing pressure and size rather than by writing better — **Moderate** (n=77, three age groups) — [18].
21. Variable-width ink is the norm — platform inks derive width from force and speed, and this app's own vector path builds a variable-width envelope of discs along the centre line. No study found that isolates the perceived-quality effect of width variation or anti-aliasing — **Product** — `StrokeOutline.swift`.
22. Pressure and tilt are platform inputs, not app-level ones: Apple Pencil reports force, altitude and azimuth, the platform inks map them to width and shading, and the app only has to preserve them. It does: the capture path records force, altitude and azimuth per sample (`CapturedInkSample`), so a replayed stroke keeps its pressure and angle — **Product** — [20], `ContinuousPagesView.swift`.
23. Hover (Pencil above the glass) is the only pre-contact signal a pen UI gets, and visual feedback plus hand posture were the two factors studied for stylus accuracy; posture mattered little for measured accuracy but changed perceived accuracy — **Moderate** — [9]. Hover is therefore best spent on *previews* (eraser footprint, snap-armed state), not on precision aids. Note the negative result: [8] hypothesised that unnatural digital postures cause inaccurate, messy content, and [9] did not find that effect — a plausible hypothesis that did not survive measurement.
24. The mode-switch ranking conflicts between studies: [11] found the non-preferred-hand button fastest and hold-still worst, while [19] found tilt and azimuth switches faster than all three conventional techniques. Weight [11]: more participants, a cleaner task, and a venue where the result has been cited and reused; [19] is a single study in a lower-tier journal. What both agree on is that *waiting* is the worst option.

## What this means for NOTEAPP

- **The latency problem is Apple's, and that is fine.** PencilKit is Apple's own low-latency engine ([21]), and the inking threshold (~50 ms, [5]) sits above what iPad + Pencil delivers. The app-level lever is *not adding* cost to the touch path: no blur or transparent layers over the live canvas, nothing synchronous in `touchesMoved`, page backgrounds off the main thread (already done — `PERFORMANCE-NOTES.md` rows 11–17). Re-rendering ink at the seen zoom (`InkCanvasHost` / `InkRenderViewport`) is the one deliberate spend of latency budget; it buys sharpness, which is the right trade, and the host already refuses to re-zoom the canvas while a tool is active (`isToolActive`: "a change waits for the tool to lift") — exactly the discipline the latency work argues for.
- **The one path we control is the first-stroke capture.** `InkCaptureRecognizer` in `ContinuousPagesView.swift` reads `coalescedTouches` but not `predictedTouches`, and draws a plain `CAShapeLayer` preview. That is exactly the case Apple documents prediction for [20]; the path is rare (a Pencil landing on a page that is still loading), so the payoff is small but the change is ~10 lines.
- **Palm rejection is OS-level, with two app-level holes.** `PencilCanvasView` sets `.pencilOnly` unless Draw with Finger is on (line 357) and the page needs two fingers to pan, which covers a resting palm. But `PencilSearchPad.swift:145` sets `.anyInput`, and the Zoom View panel is a second canvas sharing the same tool state — any finger-accepting surface on screen *while writing* is where a palm becomes a stroke. That is an audit, not a feature.
- **Mode switching already follows the evidence.** Two-finger double-tap undo, three-finger redo, Pencil double-tap, eraser auto-deselect — all "non-preferred hand or hardware" switches, the fastest class in [11]. The one place we ask the user to *wait* is Hold-to-Shape: a 500 ms hold-still (`HoldToShape.swift:78`), which is precisely the slowest, most error-prone technique in [11]. It is optional and switchable, but it should not be the only route to a clean shape.
- **Shapes are thin next to the reference set.** `ShapeRecognizer` covers line, polyline (2–4 segments), triangle, rectangle, ellipse. OneNote's draw-and-hold offers arrows, pentagons, hexagons, rhombuses, trapezoids, plus drag-to-resize/rotate *while holding* — [23]. Multi-stroke shapes (a box drawn as four lines) are not merged, and PaleoSketch shows the hierarchical route — [17]. The replacement stroke also flattens the taper on purpose ("keeps the pen's width… without the taper", `HoldToShape.swift:26`), so a snapped shape looks flatter than the line it replaced.
- **Erase is the weakest interaction.** Two eraser modes and a tool switch; no scratch-out, no hover preview of eraser size. Scratch-out is the one gesture every competitor ships and the one that removes a mode switch entirely — [23], and [11] explains why that matters.
- **Lasso exists but is bare.** `PKLassoTool` plus drag-to-move (`PencilCanvasView.swift:151`), with a hint toast. No auto-close, no sloppy-selection tolerance, no lasso-then-action phrasing — [12], [13].
- **Zoom View is a solid DIZI implementation with one gap.** `ZoomBand` gives 1.5–5× (default 2.5×), 60% advance, 20% advance area, 5% margins, 350 ms advance delay; the geometry matches GoodNotes' published behaviour and exceeds DIZI's validated 2.0× default. The gap: margins and return height are page-global fractions, while GoodNotes lets the return height be set **per template** — [23]. Ruled paper at a 5% margin will not line up with the ruling.
- **Nothing in the app knows about handedness, hover or squeeze.** Grep turns up no code that reads handedness, Pencil hover or squeeze — the only `hover` hits are comments about drag-and-drop, and the only `handed` hits are "handed to" in prose. Left-handed stylus users adopt different grips and need mirrored rejection regions — [6], [8] — and the toolbar, palettes, Zoom View panel and lasso hint are all laid out for a right hand.
- **Pressure and tilt are already where they belong.** `InkTool` carries style, colour and a nominal width only; PencilKit derives width and shading from force, altitude and azimuth per ink type, and the capture path preserves all three. The one app-level lever would be a per-pen width curve, which PencilKit does not expose — see Unverified.
- **Not worth building: a smoothing/stabilisation slider.** PencilKit does not expose its ink smoothing, and a second smoother on the capture path would only affect the rare first-stroke preview. Similarly, style-preserving beautification [15] needs a large corpus of the same glyphs before it beats doing nothing.

### Already done — do not rebuild

- **Low-latency ink on the main canvas** (PencilKit, Metal, prediction inside the framework): ALREADY DONE by the platform; only the capture path is ours (#12).
- **Sharp ink at any zoom** without re-rendering under the Pencil (`InkCanvasHost.isToolActive`): ALREADY DONE, and it is the right latency trade.
- **Hold-to-shape** for line / rectangle / triangle / ellipse, plus PencilKit's ruler (`isRulerActive`, mutually exclusive with hold-to-shape): ALREADY DONE; the gaps are #2, #3, #4.
- **Fast mode switches**: two-finger double-tap undo, three-finger redo, Pencil double-tap (system / eraser / previous tool / palette / off), eraser auto-deselect: ALREADY DONE, and they are the fastest class in [11].
- **Zoom View**: magnification 1.5–5×, 60% advance, 20% advance area, wrap to the next line and page, auto-advance 350 ms after lift, frame drag and resize: ALREADY DONE; the gaps are #10 and #11.
- **Palm rejection while writing**: `.pencilOnly` unless Draw with Finger is on, two-finger pan so a resting palm cannot scroll: ALREADY DONE; the gap is the audit in #8.

Not proposed: a smoothing/stabilisation slider. PencilKit does not expose its ink smoothing, and a second, competing smoother on the capture path would only affect the rare first-stroke preview (see Unverified).

## Unverified

- **Apple Pencil Pro squeeze / barrel roll.** I could not open the `UIPencilInteraction` or hover-pose documentation this session (developer.apple.com returned no content, then 404). The capability exists, but I have no source for the exact API or its availability on iOS 27. Idea #13 rests on it.
- **PencilKit internals.** Apple documents neither whether `PKCanvasView` uses predicted touches internally, nor whether ink smoothing or the force→width curve is configurable. That is why the smoothing-slider idea was dropped rather than listed. Related: I opened only Apple's predicted-touches page [20], not the `UITouch` force / `altitudeAngle` / `azimuthAngle` reference, so finding 22 rests on the platform behaviour visible in the app's own code (`CapturedInkSample`) as much as on documentation.
- **Palm rejection inside iPadOS.** Proprietary and undocumented. The palm claims above concern *which of our views accept finger input*, not the system classifier.
- **Lasso vs rectangle, controlled comparison.** Mizobuchi & Yasumura, *Tapping vs. Circling Selections on Pen-based Devices* (CHI 2004, 607–614) is cited by [12] and [13] but I did not open it; no effect sizes are quoted here.
- **Scratch-out accuracy or speed.** No controlled study found — only product documentation [23] and [11]'s passing mention that Windows Journal supports scratching out a word. Idea #1 is justified by the mode-switch cost, not by scratch-out's own measured performance.
- **Paper-like screen protectors.** Only product reviews and one Japanese workshop paper (IPSJ HCI 2025, pen-tip friction vs handwriting "blur") surfaced; no peer-reviewed measurement of a paper-like film's effect on handwriting quality or speed. Do not quote a number for it.
- **Haptic/audio feedback improving handwriting.** No study found linking pen-on-paper sound or haptics to measured handwriting quality. Idea #17 is speculative.
- **Left-handed layout gains.** A 2026 MDPI *Engineering Proceedings* paper reports 18–22% reach reduction and fewer gesture misfires for mirrored/adaptive layouts, but I saw only a search snippet and did not open it; treated as weak and not quoted in the findings.
- **Pen tilt/pressure as interaction channels.** Tian et al. (tilt menus) and Bi et al. (pen rolling, UIST '08) appeared only as citations in search results; I did not open them. Idea #13 does not depend on them.

## Top 3 to build first

**1. Scratch-out erase.** It is the only idea here that *removes* a mode switch instead of adding one, and [11] is unambiguous that explicit mode switches cost time and errors: reaching for the eraser is a switch on every correction, while a scribble stays inside the pen tool. The reference apps all ship the gesture [23]. It is self-contained — a stroke observer like `StrokeHoldObserver`, a coverage test against `session.visibleStrokes`, one undo step — and it fails safe, because a false positive is one undo away. **2. Hold-to-shape without the wait, plus the wider shape set and the taper.** The feature already exists and already matches the competitor concept; its measured weakness is the 500 ms hold-still, the worst technique in [11]. Recognising on lift with a ghost and a tap removes the wait, the extra shapes close the gap to OneNote's eleven [23], and keeping the taper fixes the one visible quality regression the current code introduces deliberately. **3. Lasso that closes itself.** Lasso is the documented right selection tool for ink [12], [13], the app already has it, and the missing 10% — auto-close, sloppy tolerance, move without a tool change — is what makes it usable mid-sentence. Cheap, and it retires the hint toast. Left-handed mode is a strong fourth but conditional: build it only if the person writes left-handed, as a one-time prompt rather than a default.
