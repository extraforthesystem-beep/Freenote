# Active reading, PDF annotation, reading on screens, focus and accessibility

Researched 2026-11-13. Scope: what the literature says about reading-with-a-pen, annotation, reading off paper vs screen, and the accessibility constraints that follow. Codebase checked narrowly: `NoteApp/Import`, `NoteApp/Export`, `NoteApp/Notebook/PDFLinkHandler.swift`, `NoteApp/Notebook/PageBackground.swift`, the dark-paper ADR, `Packages/StudyKit`, `Packages/SearchKit`.

## Key findings

Each finding: claim — evidence strength — source.

1. **Active reading is deliberate work, not exposure.** Adler's four levels (elementary, inspectional, analytical, syntopical) frame reading as questioning, outlining and marking; "analytical reading is always intensely active". XLibris adopted this definition as its design premise. — Conceptual/authoritative, not empirical — [1][2]

2. **Paper annotation separates into *form* (within-text marks, marginalia, interlinear notes, doodles) and *function* (procedural signalling, placemarking/memory, problem-working in situ, interpretation, trace of attention, incidental).** The same form serves several functions, so a form→function map is many-to-many. — Observational, 15 course areas / 150+ used textbooks — [3]

3. **Annotations have real value to later readers, but unevenly.** Interpretive marginalia and worked solutions were valued by the next owner of a used textbook; pure highlighting was the least valued and the most often treated as "damage control" (students buying used books explicitly asked for marginalia *without* highlighting). — Observational — [3]

4. **Paper annotation is fast, interleaved with reading and idiosyncratic; on-line annotation with mouse/keyboard was cumbersome and broke the flow.** Subjects wanted marks (a) on the source, (b) as a *separate layer* that does not modify the document, and (c) *perceptually distinct* from the text so the eye can find them while skimming. — Lab study, 5 subjects per condition (small) — [4]

5. **Tool switching costs annotation.** Students who used highlighters wrote fewer marginal notes than students who underlined with a pen; Marshall attributes this to the attention cost of switching implements and to illegible highlighter writing. The design implication is explicit: annotate with the tool already in hand, freeform, *not* a palette of predefined symbols and colours. — Observational, confounded by legibility — [3]

6. **Screen reading is slightly worse for comprehension, and the effect is specific.** Delgado et al.: g = −0.21 (56 between-subject and 18 within-subject effect sizes, 171,055 participants); larger under time pressure, present for informational text, absent for narrative. Clinton: g = −0.25 (33 randomised studies, n = 2,799), expository g = −0.32, narrative g = −0.04; no difference in reading time. Schwabe et al.: no significant medium effect for narrative text. — Strong (three converging meta-analyses) — [5][6][7]

7. **Screen readers are more overconfident, not just slightly worse.** Calibration of comprehension was better on paper, g = +0.20 (11 studies, n = 698): people spend the same time, understand slightly less, and believe they understood more. — Moderate–strong — [6]

8. **Highlighting is a low-utility learning technique; retrieval is high-utility.** Dunlosky et al. rated highlighting/underlining, summarisation, rereading, keyword mnemonic and imagery as *low* utility; practice testing and distributed practice as *high*. Highlighting "does not consistently boost performance". — Strong review of the evidence — [8]

9. **Space is the binding constraint for annotation, and readers want it next to the anchor.** In SpaceInk, participants mostly wrapped notes in the paragraph (36.4%) or inserted between lines (34.2%), less often between words (16.9%) or at the paragraph side (12.6%); they could not estimate how much space a note would need, so "make space *before* writing" techniques wasted space or overflowed. — Controlled study, 12 participants — [9]

10. **Making space beats using space that already exists, and the placement matters more than the speed.** TextTearing: direct annotation in existing whitespace was the *least* preferred condition even though it was fastest (0.65 s vs 1.96 s pen-tearing); a participant explained that space created *below* the target line produced less hand occlusion than writing in the side margin. Margin-only expansion was mid-pack (preference rank 3.17 of 5) and took 1.19 s; pen-only one-handed tearing won on preference despite being ~3× slower than direct writing. — Controlled study, 12 participants — [10]

11. **Annotations with persistent two-way links become navigation, not just records.** LiquidText let comments refer to several passages and excerpts keep a link back to source; comments were used by 10 of 18 participants for summarising, noting contradictions and cross-project relations. Excerpts were dragged out of the document into a workspace and tinted at the source. — Formative evaluation, 18 participants — [11]

12. **Paper's advantage over screen was spatial layout and interleaving, not resolution.** O'Hara & Sellen explicitly note none of the paper benefits were about resolution, contrast or viewing angle: they were annotation-while-reading, fast navigation, and laying pages/notes out side by side so reading and writing interleave. — Lab study, small n — [4]

13. **Any interruption costs, even a related one.** Interrupted work was completed faster with no quality loss, but with higher workload, stress, frustration, time pressure and effort; sharing context with the interrupted task did not help. — Controlled lab — [12]

14. **Positive polarity (dark text on light background) is more legible than dark mode.** Visual acuity and proofreading were better with positive polarity for both younger and older adults; the mechanism is display luminance (brighter screen → smaller pupil → sharper retinal image), not familiarity. — Two experiments (acuity, proofreading) plus a pupil-size follow-up — [13]

15. **"Dyslexia fonts" do not work; letter spacing does.** OpenDyslexic showed no improvement in reading rate or accuracy vs Arial/Times New Roman in dyslexic students. The Dyslexie font showed no benefit in 170 dyslexic children (passages) or in 102+45 children (word lists), and children preferred Arial. Extra-large letter spacing substantially improved text reading in a large unselected sample of Italian and French dyslexic children, attributed to reduced crowding. — Moderate (fonts), moderate–strong (spacing) — [14][15][16]

16. **Minimum target size matters for motor accessibility.** WCAG 2.2 SC 2.5.8 requires 24×24 CSS px (or equivalent spacing), and names hand tremor, spasticity, quadriplegia, stylus imprecision and one-handed use as the beneficiaries; 2.5.5 uses 44×44. — Normative standard — [17]

## What this means for NOTEAPP

- **The screen deficit is small and it is a *process* deficit.** g ≈ −0.2 is not "screens are bad"; Delgado et al. put it at about two-thirds of a year's growth in elementary-school comprehension, and it disappears for narrative. The three process affordances that paper won on in 1997 — mark without breaking flow, move between places fast, put two things side by side — are exactly what a note app controls. That is the target, not "paper feel".
- **We already have the paper-parity structural wins.** Ink is a separate layer above the PDF element (`PageElement.pdfPage` + page strokes); marks are perceptually distinct (translucent highlighter, `EditorToolState.highlighterAlpha = 0.35`); search spans PDF text, typed text and ink (`SearchKit`); outline, bookmarks, split view and Read Only exist. Marshall's "independent layer" and "perceptually distinct" requirements are satisfied by construction — do not re-litigate them.
- **The missing half is space.** A PDF page is an element at 0,0 sized to the media box (`PDFImporter.makePage`, line 326). The writable margin is whatever the publisher left. Every space study says the same thing: readers want room *at the anchor*, they cannot predict how much, and creating it is worth a slower gesture. Adding margin at import (and per page later) is the single highest-value structural change for PDF reading, and `Page.width`/`Page.height` being per-page means no format change is required — only the element frame offset.
- **Capture is over-built relative to retrieval.** There is highlight ink, search-hit marks, outline titles and bookmarks, but no list of *what I marked in this book*, no link from a mark back to its source, and no route from a highlight into recall. Marshall's later-reader evidence and XLibris's Reader's Notebook both point at retrieval, and Dunlosky says the capture act itself buys little. Building the return path is worth more than a better highlighter.
- **Do not chase annotation taxonomies.** Marshall argues against a palette of common symbols/colours; users invent personal systems only as elaborate as they can afford. A symbol picker or "annotation type" system would fight the evidence. Freeform ink plus optional colour is correct; the useful metadata is *where it came from*, not *what kind it is*.
- **Dark paper is a comfort feature, not a legibility feature.** The ADR's "white paper unless Dark Paper is on" matches the polarity evidence; keep the default. During this pass I noticed a real gap: `PaperAppearance.image` recolours the paper and inverts ink but draws elements "in true colour", so an imported PDF page stays a full-bright white rectangle on #1E1E1E — which is the worst combination for glare. Dim the PDF paper (no text inversion) rather than leaving it white.
- **Accessibility that is cheap and evidenced:** spacing/line-height presets (not dyslexia fonts), 44 pt targets, and a mirrored left-handed layout. **Accessibility that is expensive and unevidenced:** VoiceOver on raw ink. The honest route there is exposing recognised text and annotation summaries as accessibility elements, not trying to make strokes navigable.

## Top 3 to build first

**1. Add writing margin at PDF import (#1), then per page (#9).** It is the only change that addresses the measured structural gap — the writable space around the text — and it needs no new document format, because page size is already per page and the PDF is just an element with a frame. It also makes every later annotation feature (recap, backlinks, excerpts) land in space that exists.

**2. Annotation recap plus backlink (#2 + #3).** The app can already make marks and can already find text under them via `SearchKit`'s PDF marks; what is missing is the return path. A per-notebook list of marks with a snippet and a tap-to-source jump turns scattered ink into something rereadable, and it is the same infrastructure the excerpt feature (#5) will need — so it de-risks the expensive item.

**3. Highlights → study cards (#4).** This is the cheapest way to convert a low-utility activity into a high-utility one: the highlight stays as the placemarker it is good at, and the card adds retrieval practice. `StudyCard` already carries `sourceNotebookID`/`sourcePageID` and has no UI using them, so the work is editor and review-flow, not model.

Deliberately not first: excerpt capture (#5) is the most valuable single feature on the list but needs PDF text selection, a new provenance store and a target-page picker — it should follow the backlink work rather than lead it.

## Unverified

- **"Fluid annotations"** — I could not find or open a paper with this title. The closest verified works are LiquidText [11], TextTearing [10] and SpaceInk [9]; the term should not be cited as a paper until the reference is produced.
- **VoiceOver on handwritten ink** — no study found on making raw strokes navigable by screen reader. The claim that this is hard is engineering inference from PencilKit's opaque stroke model, not a sourced finding. Feature #17 is therefore accessibility engineering, not evidence-based.
- **Left-handed writing on tablets** — no peer-reviewed source opened on mirroring layouts or smudge/occlusion rates by handedness. #14 rests on TextTearing's occlusion remark [10] and on competitor behaviour (MyScript's handed zoom-chip placement, recorded in `research_notes/competitors/others/SURVEY.md`), which is weak evidence.
- **Motor impairment and handwriting recognition** — no study opened. The only normative source is WCAG target size [17]; claims about tremor filtering or stroke smoothing improving recognition are unsupported here.
- **Reading aloud / TTS and comprehension** — no study opened this session; no claim made about learning benefit.
- **Line length and margins for on-screen reading** — not researched; #1's margin width should be a user choice rather than a "correct" value from the literature.
- **Exact effect sizes under time pressure** — Delgado et al. report the moderator as significant but I did not extract the per-moderator effect size; the finding is stated qualitatively.
