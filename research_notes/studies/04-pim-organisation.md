# Personal information management, organisation and re-finding

Scope: folders vs tags vs search, re-finding behaviour, spatial memory and canvas vs paged layout, links
and backlinks, landmarks and overviews, recency / jump-back, quick capture, templates, archiving, and
audio-synced notes. Evidence strength is my read of each paper; where a claim is an extrapolation to
NOTEAPP it says so.

## Key findings

**Filing vs searching**

1. **Folder navigation is the dominant retrieval method and it is fast.** 296 participants retrieving
   1,131 of their own files (5,035 navigation steps): 94% success, 14.76 s mean, mean retrieval depth 2.86
   folders, ~11.8 files per folder with ~10.6 subfolders. Shallow, small folders were *adaptive*, not lazy —
   both depth and folder size predicted slower, less successful retrieval. — Strong (largest study of its
   kind, self-reported file set) — [1]

2. **Elaborate folder structures do not buy better re-finding.** Field study, 345 users, >85,000 refinding
   actions in an instrumented mail client with folders, tags, search and threading: heavy filers relied on
   their folders but their preparation was inefficient and did **not** improve retrieval success; search and
   threading did. Scrolling was the most prevalent refinding behaviour and is ignored by search-first
   clients. — Strong for email; extrapolation to handwritten notebooks is mine — [2]

3. **Better search does not replace browsing.** Two questionnaire studies (Windows/Google Desktop vs XP
   Search; Spotlight vs Sherlock): users estimated navigation for 56–68% of retrievals and search for only
   4–15%; improving the search engine had limited, inconsistent effects; search was a last resort when the
   location could not be recalled; nobody changed filing habits because search got better. — Strong
   (large N, two platforms, self-report) — [3]

4. **Directed search is usually "orienteering", not one perfect query.** Diary study across email, files
   and web: most directed search was *not* keyword search — people took small local steps, using contextual
   knowledge (a folder, a date, a nearby item) to narrow in, even when they knew the target in advance. This
   was most pronounced for participants with unstructured organisation. — Moderate–strong (diary, modest N) — [4]

5. **People do not reliably reproduce their own words.** Spontaneous naming studies in five domains: the
   chance that two people pick the same term for the same object was <20% in every domain; single-word
   access produced 80–90% failure rates. — Strong, classic (but between-people; within-person drift over
   time is the analogous risk here) — [5]

6. **Re-finding, not discovery, is a huge share of search.** One-year query log (114 users) plus a survey
   (119): up to **40% of queries are re-finding queries**. — Strong (logs) — [6]

**Tags**

7. **Attitudes to tags and behaviour with tags diverge.** Questionnaire (N=168) from the same population as
   an earlier behavioural study: people *say* tags and multiple classification are better ("folders are just
   habit", "children will use tags"), while the behavioural study showed a clear preference for folders and
   single classification. — Moderate (self-report vs behaviour, same population) — [7]

8. **Tags work as a secondary channel, alongside folders and threads — not instead of them.** Design and
   early field input on a mail client combining tags, threads and folders; tags were used for cross-cutting
   retrieval while folders stayed the main structure. — Weak–moderate (design paper / early user input) — [8]

**Spatial memory, canvas, overviews**

9. **Arranging items yourself in space beats a flat list.** Data Mountain: users placed documents on an
   inclined plane; retrieval showed a statistically reliable advantage over Internet Explorer Favorites for
   managing documents in an information workspace. — Moderate–strong (lab study, one task family) — [9]

10. **Location and place cues measurably improve memory.** Infocockpit: multiple surrounding displays plus
    ambient visual/auditory context produced a **56% increase in memory for information** versus a standard
    desktop. — Moderate (single lab study, small N) — [10]

11. **Wayfinding in large spaces depends on environmental cues.** Experiment in virtual worlds with varying
    cues: without added cues subjects were often disoriented and had extreme difficulty completing a search
    task; the cues present strongly shaped the strategies people used. — Moderate (lab, virtual worlds) — [11]

12. **Large space helps sensemaking specifically.** Two studies on large high-resolution displays: the
    spatial environment became part of the distributed cognitive process — external memory plus a semantic
    layer for relationships. — Moderate (two studies; effect sizes not extracted here) — [12]

13. **A whole-document thumbnail overview beats scrolling for page finding.** Space-Filling Thumbnails:
    faster than seven scrolling techniques (n=13) and faster than thumbnail-enhanced scrollbars across all
    document types and lengths (n=32, n=14), *especially for revisitation*; strongly preferred. — Strong
    (three experiments) — [13]

14. **Paged media afford lightweight, approximate navigation that screens tend to lose.** Observational study
    of magazine reading: unselfconscious in-article navigation and "flip several pages at once" are routine
    on paper and absent in the digital equivalents. — Moderate (observational) — [14]

15. **Paper's advantage in reading is annotation + quick navigation + spatial layout.** Lab comparison:
    these three let readers build a sense of structure, cross-refer, and interleave reading and writing. —
    Moderate (lab) — [15]

16. **Readers remember where information sat on the page.** Incidental memory for location of information
    in text, and memory for where words appeared in prose. — Classic lab result; I verified metadata only
    (see Unverified) — [17][18]

17. **Keeping the same visual representation across search and revisitation helps.** Visual snippets study:
    consistency of page representation across tasks matters, not just the quality of one representation. —
    Moderate — [19]

**Recency, history, "jump back"**

18. **Revisitation is pervasive and patterned.** Five weeks of logs from >612,000 users: four distinct
    revisitation patterns with different behavioural and content signatures. — Strong (logs) — [20]

19. **Browser revisit facilities are under-used because their models are inconsistent/disparate** —
    back, history and bookmarks each behave differently; integrating them around recency helps. — Moderate
    (journal study + CHI EA thesis) — [16][21]

20. **Bookmarks accumulate into a personal web space that is organised ad hoc.** The CHI study is titled
    "personal web space construction and organization"; I verified metadata only, so this is a title-level
    claim about growth and ad-hoc organisation, not a measured failure rate. — Weak — [22]

21. **A unified "everything I have seen" index with rich contextual cues works in real use.** Stuff I've
    Seen: one index over mail, files, web and calendar, with time, people and previews as search cues,
    deployed and used. — Moderate — [23]

**Scraps and piles**

22. **Small information lives outside the tools built for it.** 27 knowledge workers, 5 organisations:
    scraps on paper, in self-addressed email, in loose text files; roles were temporary storage, archiving,
    work-in-progress, reminding and unusual data; unmet needs were lightweight entry, unconstrained content,
    flexible use. — Moderate (qualitative) — [24]

23. **Desk organisation is mainly a reminding device, and categorising is cognitively expensive.** Interview
    study: the important function of desk layout is remembering to do things, not finding them; people keep
    untitled "piles" next to titled "files" precisely because classification is hard. — Moderate
    (interviews) — [25]

**Audio + notes**

24. **Note-taking activity is a good index into speech.** Audio Notebook field study: structuring recordings
    by note-taking events (plus acoustic structure) supported usage styles from detailed review to
    high-speed skimming; the study motivated phrase detection and topic suggestions. — Moderate (field
    study) — [26]

25. **Handwritten notes as the access path to recorded conversation is an old idea that keeps being
    reinvented.** Filochat (1994) predates Notability's Note Replay by two decades; the app-side equivalent
    already exists in this repo as competitor research (tap a word/sketch during playback to hear what was
    said then; new ink is timestamped). — Weak–moderate (precedent + product baseline; Filochat's measured
    results were not read) — [27][28]

**Templates**

26. **Partial-outline "guided notes" improve note accuracy and performance.** Meta-analysis: effective and
    socially valid, particularly for school-age students. — Moderate, but education outcomes (recall/test
    performance), not personal re-finding — [29]

**Links, backlinks, Zettelkasten**

27. **Luhmann's card index is the canonical "networked notes" story.** The paper describes it as an apparatus
    that fabricated serendipity rather than a database that answered queries. It is a historical/descriptive
    account of one scholar's practice, not an experiment, and the specific linking mechanics (keyword index,
    cross-references) are background I did not verify in the text I opened. — Historical — [30]

28. **Empirical evidence for bidirectional links is thin.** A 36-student study of Obsidian in higher education
    found that learners' organisation strategies were strongly individual, and that matching the strategy to
    the person improved use; its reported result concerns organisation preferences, not a measured backlink
    effect. A 2025 case study of industry researchers' "second brains" is qualitative and I could not read its
    abstract. — Weak — [31][32]

**Archiving**

29. **Archiving is not only retrieval.** 48 academics: keeping rationales include legacy, sharing resources,
    managing anxiety, and identity construction, and these shaped what was kept and how. — Moderate
    (qualitative) — [33]

30. **Email is used as an attention/task surface, not just an archive** — the original "email overload"
    finding: volume, task management and inbox-as-to-do-list, with filing behaviour that does not
    necessarily serve retrieval. — Moderate, classic — [34]

## What this means for NOTEAPP

- **Do not build a tag-first app.** Tags already exist in the data model (`FolderTree.tags`) and in the UI
  only as a comma-separated dialog plus three chips on a card — that is exactly the "attitudes without
  behaviour" pattern in [7]. Tags will earn their place only if they are *browsable* (a sidebar row with
  counts) and *filterable in search*, i.e. a second axis on top of folders, not a replacement.
- **Folders are already correct.** Shallow, small folders with 12-level depth guard (`AppModel.maxFolderDepth`)
  match what [1] says works. Nothing to fix; do not add deep hierarchy affordances.
- **The retrieval path to optimise is browsing with cues, not search quality.** [3][4] say the user is
  orienteering: notebook → page → region. That means the highest-value work is *surfacing where things are*
  (thumbnails, titles, bookmarks, recents), not better recognition. SearchKit is already good; its results
  are text-only (`SearchHit.snippet`), which is the weakest link.
- **The missing "jump back" surface is the biggest gap.** Recents today = 12 most recently *edited notebooks*
  (`AppModel.visibleNotebooks`), and the only page-level memory is one `lastVisitedPageIndex` per notebook.
  There is no "the six pages I was writing on this week", no per-notebook back/forward across pages, and no
  board/notebook cross-history. [20][21][23] all point at this.
- **Audio replay is the best value-per-effort feature in the repo.** `AudioKit` already contains the hard
  part — `AudioAnchor` (monotonic-clock-safe offset maths), `StrokeRecord.createdAt`, and
  `PlaybackController.playbackOffset(forStrokeID:)` — but no app code calls `PlaybackController`, and
  `NotebookAudio` only plays/seeks a whole clip. Wiring tap-ink → seek is a UI job on finished infrastructure,
  and it is the one feature competitors advertise heavily ([28]) with 25 years of supporting research
  ([26][27]).
- **Canvas is for synthesis, pages are for writing.** [12] supports space for sensemaking; [13][14] support
  paged, overview-navigable documents for finding things. BoardKit is currently a sealed world — items are
  ink/image/text/shape/connector (`BoardItemContent`), with no reference to a notebook or page — so boards
  cannot yet hold "the map of my notes". That is the single most valuable structural change, and the one with
  the least direct evidence for *re-finding* (spatial benefits are lab-demonstrated, not field-demonstrated
  for note apps).
- **Backlinks are a low-priority bet.** [30] is historical, [31] tested organisation preferences rather than
  links, and [11] says link-rich spaces disorient without landmarks. If links are added, they must be few and
  always paired with a human-readable list (backlinks at the bottom of the page), never a graph view as the
  primary navigation.
- **Quick capture is missing and matters more than organisation.** [24][25]: scraps exist because filing
  costs a decision. Every capture path in NOTEAPP today forces a decision (new notebook → title → folder).
  A one-tap scrap into a fixed Inbox notebook is small and evidence-backed.
- **Templates are a cheap, honest win** ([29]), but the evidence is about learning from lectures, so treat
  "guided note" templates as writing scaffolds, not as a re-finding feature.

## Unverified

- **Abstracts I did not see (metadata and titles only):** Rothkopf 1971 [17], Lovelace & Southall 1983
  [18], Tauscher & Greenberg 1997 [16] (abstract unavailable; used only to mark the existence of the
  history/revisitation design literature, never for a number), Abrams et al. 1998 [22] (only the ACM landing
  page text; the finding is explicitly title-level), Filochat [27] (abstract page returned page chrome, not
  the abstract — the claim I make is limited to what the title states), Whittaker & Sidner 1996 [34].
- **Obsidian "second brains" case study [32]:** no abstract available on the publisher record I opened; I
  cite it only as "a qualitative 2025 case study exists", not for any finding.
- **Effect sizes:** for Andrews et al. [12] and Stifelman et al. [26] I report the qualitative conclusion
  from their abstracts; I did not extract the numeric results from the PDFs.
- **Pen vs keyboard:** I deliberately excluded Mueller & Oppenheimer (2014, *Psychological Science*,
  doi:10.1177/0956797614524581). That finding is contested and I did not verify the replication/
  meta-analysis literature this session, so it should not be used to justify product decisions here.
- **Numbers I could not confirm and therefore did not use:** revisitation percentage from Tauscher &
  Greenberg; Tauscher/Greenberg history-design effects; "58% of pages are revisits"; any claim that
  backlinks improve retrieval speed.
- **Extrapolations flagged in the text:** [2] is email, [9][10] are lab placement tasks, [11] is virtual
  worlds, [29] is classroom learning. None of them measured retrieval in a handwritten note app. The
  strongest direct evidence for NOTEAPP's domain is [1][3][4] (personal files) and [13][14][15] (documents
  and pages), which is why those drive the top of the table.

## Top 3 to build first

**1. Tap-ink audio replay.** It is the only feature here where the research is old and consistent
([26][27]), the competitor baseline is explicit ([28]), and the hard engineering is already committed in
`AudioKit` (`AudioAnchor`, `PlaybackController.playbackOffset(forStrokeID:)`, `StrokeRecord.createdAt`) with
no app code using it — so the work is UI and state, not new signal processing. **2. A real "continue"
surface plus page-level jump-back.** [20][21][23] say revisitation dominates; today the app remembers one
page per notebook and sorts "Recent" by edit time, so returning to *what you were writing* is guesswork.
Both are small, local changes to `AppModel`/`NotebookSession` and pay off every session. **3. Browsable tags
and thumbnail search results.** [7] warns that tags die as a write-only field (which is exactly their state
now), while [19][23] say the result *representation* is what makes re-finding work; together they convert two
already-built data sources (tags in `FolderTree`, snippets in `SearchHit`) into retrieval. Page-overview grid
(#4) is the natural fourth: strongest evidence in the whole set ([13]), but a bigger UI build.
